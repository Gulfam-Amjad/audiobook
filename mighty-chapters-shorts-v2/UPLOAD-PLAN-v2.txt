TITLE: Apollo 13: The Square Peg That Nearly Killed Three Men

DESCRIPTION:
In April 1970, an oxygen tank exploded on Apollo 13. The crew moved into the lunar module, Aquarius, and carbon dioxide began to build up. The command module's filters were round, and Aquarius's slot was square. Engineers on the ground built an adapter from plastic bags, duct tape, cardboard, and a spacesuit hose. The crew built it and came home on April 17, 1970.

TITLE: The Astronaut Who Almost Drowned in Space

DESCRIPTION:
On July 16, 2013, Italian astronaut Luca Parmitano was outside the International Space Station, about 250 miles up. Water from his suit's cooling system leaked and drifted into his helmet. He could not see, and water was close to his face. His partner Chris Cassidy helped guide him back to the airlock, and NASA called off the spacewalk to investigate the leak.

TITLE: He Walked in Space First, Then Died in a Fire

DESCRIPTION:
On June 3, 1965, Ed White became the first American to walk in space. He was tethered to Gemini 4 and moved with a handheld gun that fired bursts of oxygen. After about twenty-one minutes, mission control called him back in. On January 27, 1967, White died in a fire inside the Apollo 1 capsule during a launch rehearsal, along with Gus Grissom and Roger Chaffee.

Photos: NASA (public domain), including Ed White and Apollo 1 images from Wikimedia Commons (public domain).

#4  FILE: 04-the-doctor-who-washed-his-hands.mp4   (cover: 04-the-doctor-who-washed-his-hands-cover.jpg)
TITLE: The Doctor Who Was Ignored for Saying Wash Your Hands

DESCRIPTION:
In 1847, Ignaz Semmelweis worked in Vienna's General Hospital, where women in the doctors' maternity ward died of childbed fever far more often than in the midwives' ward. He noticed that the doctors came straight from autopsies. When a colleague died after cutting himself during an autopsy, Semmelweis ordered doctors to wash their hands in chlorinated lime. Deaths in the first ward dropped sharply. Many senior doctors rejected his idea. He died in 1865.

Photos: Wikimedia Commons (public domain).

#5  FILE: 05-the-iron-rod.mp4   (cover: 05-the-iron-rod-cover.jpg)
TITLE: The Iron Rod That Went Through a Man's Head, and He Lived

DESCRIPTION:
On September 13, 1848, Phineas Gage was a railroad foreman in Vermont. An explosion drove an iron tamping rod through his left cheek and out the top of his skull. He survived, but friends said he was no longer himself. Doctors studied him for years. His case became an early clue that personality depends on the brain. He died in 1860. The rod is kept at Harvard.
