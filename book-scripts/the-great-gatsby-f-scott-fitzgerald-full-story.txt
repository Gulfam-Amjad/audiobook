# Introduction

Welcome to Mighty Chapters. This is the complete story of The Great Gatsby, the novel F. Scott Fitzgerald published in nineteen twenty-five.

The book is in the public domain. This is an original retelling, in new words, made for listening. It follows the whole plot. Nick's summer, the parties, Daisy, the hotel, the garage, the funeral, and the last page are all here.

The story is told by Nick Carraway, a young man from the Middle West who believes he is the most honest person he knows. He is decent. He is also close enough to the trouble to be changed by it, and far enough away to write it down afterward. Everything you hear has passed through his eyes.

# West Egg

In the spring of nineteen twenty-two Nick leaves his family in the Midwest and goes east to learn the bond business. He rents a small house on Long Island, at West Egg, the less fashionable of two bulges of land that face each other across a bay. His house is a cottage squeezed beside a mansion. The mansion belongs to a man he has not met, Jay Gatsby, who gives parties so large that the lights look like a city.

Across the water is East Egg, where the houses are older and the money pretends it has always been there. Nick's cousin Daisy Buchanan lives on that side with her husband, Tom. Tom was a football star at Yale, rich, cruel in a careless way, and already, in his thirties, a man whose body is starting to turn the power of his youth into bulk. Daisy has a voice people lean toward. Nick says it sounds like money.

He goes to dinner. Jordan Baker is there, a golfer, cool, a little dishonest, attractive because she seems to need nothing. The dinner is polished and wrong. Tom talks, over the food, about a book he has been reading that pretends some people are born to rule and others to be ruled. The talk is ugly. No one quite stops him. A telephone rings and Tom leaves the table too quickly. Daisy follows him out for a moment, and when she comes back her brightness is a performance with a crack in it. Jordan tells Nick, later, that Tom has a woman in the city. Everyone knows. Daisy knows.

Out on the lawn, Daisy and Nick sit in the dark and she tells him she has been everywhere and seen everything and that the best thing a girl can be in this world is a beautiful little fool. She says it about her daughter. She says it like a joke and like a wound. Across the bay, Nick sees a figure on the end of Gatsby's dock. The figure stretches out his arms toward a single green light, far away, at the end of Daisy's dock. Nick is too new to know what he is looking at. He goes home thinking the evening was strange, and that he will try not to be pulled into it.

# The Valley of Ashes

Between West Egg and New York there is a stretch of land where the city's ashes are dumped. Nick calls it the valley of ashes. Gray houses, gray air, a gray road, and above it a faded billboard of a pair of enormous eyes, the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg, left behind by an oculist who has moved on or died. The eyes look over everything and explain nothing. People in the story keep wanting them to mean God. They are paint.

Tom takes Nick there and stops at a garage. George Wilson runs the garage, a tired, washed-out man who loves his wife and is trying to buy Tom's car so he can resell it and survive. Myrtle Wilson is the wife. She walks into the garage with a body that seems too alive for the room, and Tom's manner changes. She is the woman from the telephone.

They go to the city, Tom and Myrtle and Nick, and the decency Nick likes to claim for himself takes the afternoon off. Myrtle's sister and a few neighbors crowd into an apartment Tom keeps. They drink. Myrtle talks too loudly about the life she thinks she is climbing into, and she says Daisy's name with contempt. Tom, who will betray Daisy without hesitation, will not let her be spoken of. He breaks Myrtle's nose with a short, expert blow. Blood is on the towels. The party ends the way those parties end, with Nick outside himself, half drunk, riding home through the dawn, disgusted and implicated.

He has now seen both of Tom's houses. The mansion where Daisy's voice floats over a dinner table, and the apartment where a broken nose is the price of saying the wrong thing. The same man pays for both.

# The Parties

Gatsby's parties happen almost every weekend, and they are built to be seen from across the bay.

Cars fill the drive. Crates of oranges and lemons arrive and leave as a pyramid of pulpless halves. An orchestra plays. People come who have never met the host. Rumors move through the rooms because the host is rarely in them. Some say he killed a man. Some say he was a German spy. Some say he was in the American army. Nick goes, at last, with an actual invitation, which makes him unusual. He wanders, loses Jordan, finds people who speak of Gatsby as if he might be in the next song, and meets a man with owl-eyed glasses in the library, astonished that the books are real. The owl-eyed man expected cardboard. The books are real, and many of the pages are uncut. Someone bought a library the way other people buy a tray of glasses, to complete a picture.

Late, Nick falls into a conversation with a man about his own age who speaks quietly and does not drink much. They have both been in the war. The man is Gatsby. The revelation is almost nothing, which is how Gatsby prefers to appear, and then it is everything. He smiles with a kind of concentration that makes the person smiled at feel briefly chosen. He calls Nick old sport. He says he will ask a favor later. A butler calls him to the phone, and the party closes around the space where he was.

Weeks pass. Gatsby drives Nick to the city in a cream-colored car that looks like it has never been rained on. He tells a story of himself that sounds rehearsed. Son of a wealthy family in the Middle West. Educated at Oxford. A collector of jewels, a hunter of big game, a hero of the war, decorated by Montenegro. He shows a medal and a photograph. Nick does not believe all of it and cannot quite call it a lie, because Gatsby believes it while he is saying it. In a cellar restaurant they meet Meyer Wolfsheim, a small man with cufflinks made of human molars, who fixed, Gatsby says with a kind of pride, the World Series. Wolfsheim looks at Nick as a business opportunity. Nick feels the ground under Gatsby's elegance shift. The money has a source, and the source does not look like an inheritance.

Jordan, at last, tells Nick the favor. She knew Daisy in Louisville, before the war. Daisy was a young beauty with a string of officers around her, and one of them, a lieutenant named Jay Gatsby, poor then, with no right to walk up her steps, loved her and was loved back for a while. He went overseas. She married Tom Buchanan, because Tom was there, and rich, and the world was not going to pause while a lieutenant made his fortune. The night before the wedding she almost refused. She was found with a letter in her hand, drunk, saying she had changed her mind. They put her in a cold bath. The letter fell apart. She married Tom the next day.

Gatsby has not accepted that day. He bought the mansion because it faces her dock. The parties are a net thrown over the water, in case she walks into one. The green light is her dock. He wants Nick to invite Daisy to tea. He wants the meeting to look accidental. He wants, Nick begins to understand, not merely to see her. He wants the five years since Louisville to be erased, and he believes this is possible if the tea is arranged correctly.

# The Tea

The meeting is set for a rainy afternoon at Nick's cottage. Gatsby arrives early, in a white suit, and nearly leaves. He sends a man to cut Nick's grass. He has a greenhouse's worth of flowers brought over. He stands in Nick's small rooms as if the furniture might judge him. When Daisy's car is heard in the drive he panics, goes out, and comes back in through the front as if he had only just arrived.

The first minutes are terrible. They talk about the rain. Nick leaves them and stands in the garden under an umbrella, feeling like a man who has set a fire and is now obliged to watch it. When he comes back the room has changed. Daisy has been crying. Gatsby is glowing with a disbelief that has tipped into joy. The clock on Nick's mantel has been knocked, caught, and set back in place, and the clumsiness has broken the ice in a way no speech could. They do not need Nick anymore. He understands he has been a door.

They go next door, because Gatsby cannot help showing her the house. He has shirts brought down, piles of them, silk and flannel and fine cotton, and he throws them on the table until Daisy puts her face in them and cries at the beauty and the waste and the proof that he has become someone who could have walked up her steps. He shows her the grounds, the guests' rooms, the view of the bay. He tells her that the green light at the end of her dock has been his vigil. Now that she is standing beside him, Nick thinks, the light is just a light again. The enchanted object cannot survive being touched. Gatsby does not see that yet. He thinks the touching is the beginning of the repair.

A single husbandless afternoon becomes a pattern. Daisy comes in the afternoons. The great parties continue, and then Daisy attends one, and Gatsby watches her watch the party, and he sees his own house through her eyes. She does not like it. It is too loud, too mixed, too full of people who were not in Louisville. He understands, or he thinks he understands, that the parties were only a way to find her. Now that she is found, he ends them. The servants are replaced with men who do not gossip. The house goes quiet. The quiet is for Tom's sake, and it is also a stage being cleared for a conversation Gatsby believes must happen. Daisy must tell Tom she never loved him. Then the five years will be officially canceled, and they can go back to Louisville and begin again as if the marriage were a mistake in a ledger.

Nick tries, once, to say that you cannot repeat the past. Gatsby is amazed. Of course you can, he says. And for a moment, facing that certainty, Nick almost believes him.

# Who He Was

Nick learns the true beginning, and it is plainer and braver than the Montenegro medal.

The boy's name was James Gatz. He was born to poor farming people in North Dakota, and he left them in his mind long before he left them on a train. At seventeen he was on the shore of Lake Superior when a yacht dropped anchor, owned by a copper millionaire named Dan Cody. Jimmy Gatz rowed out, warned Cody that the wind was coming, and was taken on board. He had already renamed himself Jay Gatsby, because the name sounded like the life he meant to have. Cody drank, and the boy kept him alive and kept the yacht in order for five years, sailing three times around the continent, learning how rich people move and how a fortune can be guarded or stolen. Cody died. The inheritance he meant for Gatsby was taken by a woman. The boy was left with an education in money and none of the money.

The army gave him the uniform that let him enter Daisy's house. The war gave him a silence in which she married someone else. After the war the fortune had to be made fast, because Daisy would not stay a memory forever. Wolfsheim and the business of the age, bootlegging and the darker trades that fed the parties, made it fast. Gatsby treats the criminal part as a ladder he is allowed to kick away the moment Daisy says the sentence he needs. He does not see himself as a bootlegger who loves a married woman. He sees himself as a man faithful to a promise the world interrupted.

Tom starts to ask questions. He meets Gatsby at a party and hates him on instinct, the instinct of a man who can smell a new claim on what he owns. He asks around. He and Daisy come to Gatsby's house, and the afternoon is hot, strained, and full of a politeness that is about to tear. Daisy asks, in the city, what they are all going to do with themselves, and the question hangs over the whole summer.

# The Plaza

The hottest day of the year they go to New York together. Tom drives Gatsby's car, a piece of dominance so casual he may not even taste it, and Gatsby rides with Daisy and Jordan in Tom's car. They stop for gas at Wilson's garage. Wilson looks sick. He has understood, at last, that Myrtle has a life apart from him, though he has not understood who the man is. He tells Tom he is taking Myrtle west. Tom feels his arrangement shaking, the woman in the apartment and the wife in the mansion both slipping, and the heat makes everyone a little more honest than they can afford to be.

In a suite at the Plaza Hotel they drink and the honesty arrives. Tom, who has been investigating, begins to pick Gatsby apart in front of Daisy. Oxford. The business. The nerve of a man with no family and no real money, only a car and a house full of strangers, sitting in a room with Tom Buchanan's wife. Gatsby answers some of it well. He did go to Oxford, for a few months, after the armistice, a chance the army gave some officers. The rest he will not answer, because the rest is Wolfsheim, and because he thinks the past Daisy shared with him outranks Tom's facts.

He tells Daisy to say it. Tell him you never loved him. Daisy tries. She gets as far as saying that she loved Gatsby now, that she loves him, and then Tom pushes, and she says that she did love Tom once. It is the sentence Gatsby cannot survive and cannot seem to hear. He keeps explaining, after it has been said, how she might still take it back. Daisy looks at him and sees, perhaps for the first time since the shirts, that he wants not her present but a revision of her entire life. Tom sees his opening and becomes, horribly, the safe choice. He tells Daisy to go home with Gatsby, a show of confidence, because he has already won the room. He tells them he will follow.

On the road back, in the valley of ashes, Myrtle Wilson sees the cream-colored car, the car she knows as Tom's car, coming fast. She runs toward it. She thinks Tom is in it. She has been locked upstairs by her husband, who means to take her west in the morning, and the car is her last idea of rescue. Daisy is driving. Gatsby lets her drive because she is frightened and because he would give her anything she reached for. The car hits Myrtle and does not stop. Myrtle dies in the road under the eyes of the billboard. George Wilson comes out of the garage and sees his wife, and sees the car already gone, and the world he understood ends in an afternoon.

Gatsby tells Nick, later, that Daisy was driving, and that he will say he was. He stands all night outside the Buchanan house, in the bushes, watching the windows, in case Tom hurts her. Nick looks in and sees Daisy and Tom at the table, their heads close, a plate of cold chicken between them, conspiring in the old language of their marriage. They are not separated. They are repairing the wall. Gatsby is outside, guarding a woman who has already gone back inside. Nick cannot make him leave.

# The Garage

George Wilson's grief has nowhere to go, so it goes toward God and toward a man.

He tells a neighbor that he has a way of finding out who drove the car. He stares at the eyes of Doctor Eckleburg and says God sees everything. The neighbor does not know how to answer a sentence like that. Wilson leaves the garage. He walks. He asks. He reaches Tom Buchanan, because Tom is the kind of man a garage owner would know, the man who came by in expensive cars and talked about a sale. Tom, protecting himself, tells Wilson that the yellow car was not his. He tells him whose it was. He does not tell him who was driving. He sends a broken man down the road with Gatsby's name in his mouth.

Gatsby waits in his pool. He has not left West Egg, because leaving would mean accepting that Daisy will not call. Nick, who has gone to work and hated the day, decides to tell Gatsby the truth about the cold chicken and the two heads bent together. He arrives too late to say it in time, and just in time to be the person who finds what the afternoon has done.

Wilson has found the mansion. He has found Gatsby in the pool, on a mattress, floating, still expecting a telephone to ring. He shoots Gatsby. He shoots himself.

Nick is the one who calls, the one who stays, the one who tries to make the aftermath as decent as the summer was not. Daisy and Tom have left. No address. No message. The house that was full of orchestra music is a house Nick cannot get servants or guests to care about. He tries to find people for the funeral. Wolfsheim sends word and does not come. The party guests, the hundreds who ate Gatsby's food, are suddenly people who barely knew him and would rather not be associated with a shooting. A man with owl-eyed glasses comes, the man from the library, and says, in the rain, that he could not get to the house in time, and that it was an accident, and that the poor son of a gun. It is the most honest sentence spoken over the body.

One person comes from the real past. Henry Gatz, a father, arrives from Minnesota, a small solemn man who knew his son as Jimmy and who is proud in a way that breaks Nick's composure. He brings a photograph, and a book the boy kept, a schedule written on the flyleaf. Rise at six. Exercise. Study electricity and needed inventions. Work. Read one improving book or magazine a week. Be better to parents. The handwriting is a child's, and the list is the whole novel compressed into a poor boy's morning. The father thinks the mansion is the proof that the list worked. Nick lets him think it. There is no kindness in taking that away.

# The Green Light

Nick stays until the funeral is over, and then he is done with the East.

He sees Tom once more, on a street in New York. Tom tells him, with a self-pity that makes Nick want to break something, that he told Wilson the truth as he knew it, and that Gatsby had it coming. He says he cried when he gave up the apartment. He says a thing like that is enough to make a man careful. Nick understands that Tom and Daisy were careless people. They smashed up lives, Myrtle's, Gatsby's, Wilson's, and retreated into their money, and let other people clean up the mess. Nick shakes Tom's hand anyway, because it would be silly not to, and because the time for speeches has passed. He feels that he is talking to a child.

Jordan Baker tells him, before they part, that she had thought he was an honest, careful person, and that she was wrong, or that the summer made him careless too. There is a sting in it. Nick has been the honest narrator of a story in which he arranged the meeting, went to the apartment, and stayed. He does not argue with her for long.

On his last night he walks down to Gatsby's beach. The houses are dark. The green light on Daisy's dock is visible and small. He thinks of the island as the first sailors would have seen it, green and unfinished, large enough to hold a person's biggest hope. Gatsby had believed in that light. He had believed in Daisy, who was a person, as if she were that light, and when she failed to be a symbol he had kept believing anyway, which was his greatness and the thing that killed him. Nick's last thought is the one the book is remembered for, and it earns its place only if you have heard the summer that comes before it. People go on anyway, small boats pushing against the current, carried backward, again and again, into the past they are trying to outrun.

Gatsby could not repeat the past. He also could not stop trying. The current had him, and it has the rest of them, and the light stays on the dock whether anyone swims toward it or not.

# What the Story Leaves Behind

The Great Gatsby is a short book about a long American wish. That a person can invent a name, follow a schedule, make a fortune, and arrive back at the door where love was left, and find the five missing years willing to step aside.

Daisy is not a villain in a mask, and she is not a prize that was stolen. She is a woman who once loved a poor officer and then chose safety, and who, when the officer returns impossible and rich, cannot give him the sentence that would unmake her life. Tom is cruel and also, in the Plaza, briefly the husband who knows his wife's real history. Myrtle dies because a car, a marriage, and a dream of rescue occupy the same road for one second. Wilson dies because grief was handed a name. Gatsby dies because he offered to be the driver of a car he did not drive, and because he waited in a pool for a call that was never coming.

Nick goes home to the Midwest. The parties are over. The books in the library stay uncut. The schedule in the boy's book is the truest portrait in the house. Rise, work, be better. It was never a plan for getting Daisy. It was a plan for becoming the kind of man who might deserve a green light. The novel's sadness is that he did become extraordinary, and the light was never a promise. It was a lamp on someone else's dock.

This is the end of the story of The Great Gatsby.
