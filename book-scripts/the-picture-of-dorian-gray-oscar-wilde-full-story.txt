# Introduction

Welcome to Mighty Chapters. This is the complete story of The Picture of Dorian Gray, the novel Oscar Wilde published in book form in eighteen ninety-one.

The book is in the public domain. This retelling is original, written in new words for listening, and it follows the whole plot. The portrait, the wish, Sibyl Vane, the years of secrecy, the murder, and the last cut of the knife are all here.

It is a story about a beautiful young man, a painter who loves him, a lord who talks too well, and a picture that tells the truth the young man's face refuses to tell.

# The Studio

On a summer day in London, in the studio of the painter Basil Hallward, the air smells of roses and paint. Basil has been working with a fervor he does not quite want to explain. His friend Lord Henry Wotton lounges in the studio, smoking, watching, amusing himself with other people's souls because his own life, wealthy and married and idle, does not fill him.

Basil will not show the portrait he is finishing, and then he does. It is a full-length painting of a young man of extraordinary beauty. Lord Henry is startled. Beauty, in his philosophy, is the one thing that does not need an excuse. He asks the sitter's name. Basil says the name as if it were a secret he has already failed to keep. Dorian Gray.

Basil explains, against his own caution, why he did not want Lord Henry to meet Dorian. The painter has put too much of himself into the picture. Not his face. His feeling. He says that he has painted Dorian as he really is, and that an artist who puts his soul into a work should be careful who looks at it. Lord Henry laughs at the warning in the light way that makes warnings feel provincial. He wants to meet the original.

Dorian arrives. He is about twenty, open, a little vain because people have stared at him all his life and he has not yet decided what the staring means. Lord Henry talks. This is his art, and it is more dangerous than Basil's because it leaves no object you can lock in a room. He tells Dorian that beauty is a brief kingdom, that youth is the only thing worth having, that the young waste their days in duty and dull virtue and then wake up, too late, to lines and regret. He says the world belongs to the people who do not deny themselves. Dorian listens with the flushed attention of someone hearing his own unnamed fear set to music.

Basil begs Lord Henry to stop. He can see the words landing. Dorian goes to the portrait and looks at it for a long time. He sees what he will lose. He says, half wild, that he would give his soul if the portrait would grow old and he could stay as he is in the picture. He says it like a young man's drama. The room does not shake. No one treats it as a contract. Basil signs the painting. Dorian treats the signature as if the canvas were a rival he has already started to resent.

Lord Henry leaves a book and a trail of sentences. Basil is left with the uneasy feeling of a man who has introduced his dearest subject to a corrupting guest and called it lunch.

# Sibyl Vane

Dorian falls in love, or he falls in love with a performance, which he cannot yet tell apart.

In a shabby theater he finds a young actress, Sibyl Vane, who plays Juliet and Rosalind and Imogen with a genius that makes the cheap seats irrelevant. She is seventeen, poor, fatherless in any useful sense, and she calls Dorian her Prince Charming because he watches from the boxes and will not, at first, give a name. Her mother, a faded woman with debts and vanity, is delighted by the hint of a wealthy admirer. Her brother, James Vane, a sailor about to leave for Australia, is not delighted. He tells Sibyl he will kill any man who harms her. He means it in the way a boy means a vow, and the book will remember the vow longer than the boy does.

Dorian brings his friends to see her. He has described her as a genius and as the love of his life, and he wants Basil and Lord Henry to witness the thing he has found, partly from joy and partly from the need to be admired for the finding. The night they come, Sibyl is bad. Not nervous. Bad. The poetry dies in her mouth. The gesture is wooden. The room is embarrassed for her.

Afterward she is radiant. She tells Dorian that she can no longer act, because she has learned what love is, and the painted emotions of the stage are shadows next to it. She was great when the stage was the only life she had. Now she wants a real life, with him. It is the most honest speech she ever makes, and he hears it as a humiliation. He loved Juliet. He does not love a girl who has stepped out of Juliet and asked him for ordinary devotion. He is cruel with a precision that surprises even him. He tells her she has killed his love. He leaves her weeping in the empty theater.

He walks the London night and feels, for a few hours, justified. At home he glances at the portrait. The mouth has changed. There is a touch of cruelty in it that was not there in Basil's studio. Dorian is frightened, then thrilled, then frightened again. The wish has been heard. He hides nothing yet, because he still thinks one kind act can erase a line.

Lord Henry arrives in the morning with news, delivered like a curious item from the papers. Sibyl Vane is dead. She has taken poison, or something like it, in the theater. The world will call it a sad little scandal. Lord Henry advises Dorian not to be ruined by it. The girl was less than his destiny. Suffering is a role, and Dorian should not play it too long.

Dorian is horrified, and then, under Lord Henry's voice, he does something worse than the cruelty of the night before. He accepts the idea that Sibyl's death is a beautiful tragedy in which he is the audience. He goes to the opera within days. When he looks at the portrait again, the change is no longer a trick of light. He has the picture carried upstairs to an old schoolroom, a room he locks, and he drapes it. He will not let Basil, who comes grieving and confused, see what the painting has begun to say. Basil asks for the portrait. Dorian refuses. The friendship stays. The honesty of it ends.

# The Yellow Book

Lord Henry sends Dorian a novel. It is a poisonous, exquisite book, French in spirit if not always in language, about a young man who tries to experience every sensation, to make his life into a work of art, to treat virtue and vice as colors on a palette. Dorian reads it until it becomes a script. Wilde never needs to name every sin. He shows the pattern. Years pass. Dorian does not age. London begins to talk.

The talk is ugly and fascinated. Fathers will not let him near their sons. Club doors hesitate. There are stories of ruined companions, of rooms he leaves with someone else's shame in them, of a life that looks, on the street, like perfect youth and perfect clothes. He is invited anyway, because beauty and money open doors that gossip only half closes. He collects beautiful objects. Tapestries, jewels, music, vestments, perfumes. He treats hobbies as religions and drops them when they threaten to become responsibilities. The schoolroom stays locked.

Sometimes, late, he goes upstairs, turns the key, and looks. The portrait grows loathsome. Lines appear that his mirror will not show. A stain spreads. The face becomes the diary his own face is forbidden to keep. He both hates it and needs it. It is the only true picture of him in existence, and Basil, who painted it, still believes Dorian is the innocent who sat in the summer light.

Basil comes, at last, on the night before he is to leave for Paris, and he comes with the rumors in his hands. He tells Dorian he has heard too much to stay silent. He does not want the stories to be true. He wants Dorian to deny them, so that he can go on believing the portrait was a vision of a pure soul. If the stories are true, Basil says, he must have been wrong about Dorian from the beginning, and the picture was a lie.

Dorian laughs, and the laugh frightens Basil more than a confession would. Then Dorian offers to show him the portrait. He says a man should see his work. They climb to the schoolroom. Dorian locks the door behind them and pulls the cloth away.

# The Schoolroom

Basil sees a monster wearing Dorian's features, a face corrupted beyond ordinary age, a record of everything the city has whispered and a great deal it does not know. He understands at once that the canvas is his canvas, and that no one else could have produced that change. It is not mold. It is not a joke. He begs Dorian to pray. He tells him it is never too late, that the soul can still be knelt for, that they will pray together.

Dorian's hatred of the picture turns, in that second, into hatred of the man who made it and who is now weeping in front of it. The prayer sounds like an accusation. The love in Basil's voice sounds like possession. On a table nearby there is a knife. Dorian takes it and stabs Basil Hallward again and again. The painter falls. The only person who knew Dorian before the wish, and the only person who might have stayed to help him repent, dies on the schoolroom floor, looking up at his own ruined masterpiece.

What follows is not panic of the innocent kind. Dorian's panic is practical. He sleeps, somehow. In the morning he writes to Alan Campbell, a young chemist he once called a friend, a man who has since refused to speak to him in public. He asks Campbell to come at once. Campbell comes, cold and unwilling. Dorian asks him to destroy a body so thoroughly that no one will ever find it. Campbell refuses. Dorian hands him a note, a piece of knowledge from their old intimacy, and the refusal dies. The book does not tell us the words on the note. It tells us that Campbell would rather be a murderer’s instrument than see that sentence survive. He uses the tools of his science. When he is done, the schoolroom holds no Basil. Campbell goes home and, not long after, kills himself. Dorian counts it as an unpleasant consequence and keeps moving.

He goes, that same stretch of days, to an opium den by the docks, because the portrait and the murder have begun to live behind his eyes and he wants a night with no eyes at all. In the den a woman sneers at him and calls him Prince Charming. A sailor in the corner hears the name. James Vane has come back from the sea. He has spent years with one vow. He follows Dorian into the dark street and puts a pistol to him. He says the name Sibyl Vane.

Dorian saves himself with the one fact that has never failed him. He asks to be taken under a lamp. The sailor sees a face of twenty, smooth, unlined, absurdly young. Sibyl died eighteen years ago. The man who ruined her would be middle-aged. James lets him go, shaking, convinced he has stopped the wrong person. A woman from the den runs after James and tells him he has been tricked. She says Prince Charming has looked like that for twenty years. James turns back to the street. Dorian is gone. The vow is awake again.

# The Country

Dorian retreats to his country house with a party of people who do not ask questions. He is charming at dinner. He is also listening for a step on the gravel. During a shoot, a man is killed by accident in the brush, a beater, one of the men hired to drive the birds. Dorian is told and goes, pale, to look. The dead man is James Vane. The accident has done what Dorian's fear could not finish. He feels, first, a relief so huge it sickens him, and then a thin, superstitious sense that the world has taken his side. He mistakes the accident for pardon.

He decides to be good.

The decision is real and shallow at the same time, which is why it fails. He spares a village girl, Hetty, whom he had begun to seduce. He leaves her before the harm is complete. He tells himself this is the start of a new life. He wants to see whether the portrait has softened. He imagines a line of cruelty fading because he did one decent thing and refrained from one selfish thing.

He opens the schoolroom. The face is worse. There is a new cunning in it, the look of a man who is using virtue as a disguise, who wants the credit of goodness without the cost. Even his mercy has been a kind of performance, done so that the painting would flatter him. The portrait, which has never cared about his reputation, will not be bribed by a gesture. Dorian understands that he cannot confess, because confession would mean the knife and Basil and Campbell and Sibyl all entering the record of the world. He also cannot go on being looked at by the one witness he cannot kill by ordinary means. The witness is a canvas.

He decides to destroy it. If the portrait is the record, the record can be cut to pieces. If the knife that killed the painter ends the painting, perhaps the bond breaks, and he is free, and the face in the mirror remains the only face. He takes the knife. He faces the thing he has become. He drives the blade into the canvas, into the heart of the image.

A cry rings through the house, terrible, and then silence.

# The Servants

The servants, frightened, force the door of the schoolroom. The room is locked from the inside. They break in.

On the wall hangs the portrait of Dorian Gray as Basil painted it, young, radiant, unspoiled, the face of the boy in the summer studio. On the floor lies a dead man in evening dress, a knife in his heart. He is withered, wrinkled, loathsome, a face no one in the house recognizes until they look at the rings. It is Dorian. The portrait has given back the innocence, because the soul that corrupted it is no longer using it. The body has taken, all at once, the age and the damage the picture carried for twenty years. The servants find their master only by his jewelry. The beautiful face is gone. The beautiful picture remains, which is Wilde's last, cold justice. Art survives. The man who tried to make his life a flawless surface does not.

Lord Henry is not in the room. He has spent the book believing none of this could be literal, teasing Dorian, doubting Basil, trusting conversation to be the deepest thing in the world. The ending does not give him a speech. The ending gives the servants, the rings, and the portrait restored. The clever voice that talked a boy into the wish is elsewhere, still sure of itself, while the bill is paid upstairs.

# What the Story Leaves Behind

The Picture of Dorian Gray is a fantasy with a strict moral, and Wilde, who liked to pretend morals were vulgar, built the machine carefully.

Dorian's wish splits appearance from consequence. The split is the whole temptation. He can walk into a room and be believed, because the face is a certificate of innocence, while the cost collects in a locked schoolroom. Sibyl dies because he loved a performance and punished the person for stepping out of it. Basil dies because he told the truth and offered prayer instead of applause. Alan Campbell dies of the leverage Dorian kept from an old friendship. James Vane dies in the brush, a blunt instrument of a justice that arrives late and hits the wrong hour. Hetty is spared and even that mercy is poisoned, because it was done to bargain with a painting.

The portrait is not the villain. It is the only honest face Dorian owns. When he stabs it, he is not killing a curse from outside. He is trying to kill the record, and the record was the last part of him that could still be called a soul. The knife returns the truth to the body it belonged to.

This is the end of the story of The Picture of Dorian Gray.
