# Introduction

Welcome to Mighty Chapters. This is the complete story of Frankenstein, the novel Mary Shelley published in eighteen eighteen, when she was still in her early twenties.

The book is in the public domain, which means the story itself belongs to everyone. What you are about to hear is not a copy of her sentences. It is an original retelling, written in new words for listening, that follows the whole plot from the first letter to the last scene on the ice. Nothing important is left out. The creation, the creature's own account, the deaths, the promise, and the ending are all here.

A note before we begin, so the names stay clear. The man who builds the creature is Victor Frankenstein. The creature has no name. People call the monster Frankenstein, but in the book that name belongs to the maker.

# Letters from the Ice

The story begins at the top of the world, and it begins with a man who is not Victor.

Captain Robert Walton is a young Englishman who has hired a ship and sailed north, chasing a dream he has carried since boyhood. He wants to find a passage through the polar ice, and he wants, even more than that, to stand somewhere no person has stood. He writes letters to his sister in England, Margaret Saville, because there is no one on the ship he can talk to as an equal. His crew is loyal. His lieutenant is capable. None of them share the hunger that sent him out here.

The voyage goes badly in the way polar voyages go badly. Fog. Cold that gets into the bones. A wall of ice that will not open. The men grow quiet. Walton keeps writing, half proud and half lonely, and he admits something that matters later. He has no friend. He would give up a great deal of his glory for one companion who could understand him.

Then, one morning, the lookout sees a shape on the ice where no shape should be. A sledge, drawn by dogs, and a figure huge enough that the sailors argue about the distance. The figure passes far off and is gone into the mist.

The next day they find a second sledge, broken, with only one dog left alive, and a man on it who is clearly dying. He is European, exhausted, frostbitten, and still, somehow, commanding. Walton brings him aboard, wraps him, feeds him, and watches him return from the edge of death with a strange reluctance, as if living were a task he had not asked to resume.

The stranger's name is Victor Frankenstein. As he recovers he asks Walton where the ship is bound, and when he hears "north," a terrible interest lights in him. He asks if the crew saw another sledge. He asks about a huge figure. Walton describes what the lookout saw, and Victor's face changes. He says the captain has met the person he is hunting.

They talk through the long polar evenings. Walton, hungry for a friend, tells Victor about his ambition. Victor listens with a pain that is almost anger, and then he says the words that open the rest of the book. He tells Walton that he was once the same kind of man. He tells him that the pursuit of knowledge, when it is not tied to affection and limit, can destroy the pursuer and everyone near him. And then, because he wants one person on earth to hear the warning entire, he begins his story.

Walton writes it down for his sister. Everything that follows is Victor's account, until the ice returns at the end.

# A Boy in Geneva

Victor Frankenstein is born in Geneva, into a family with money, a good name, and an unusual tenderness. His father, Alphonse, married late. His mother, Caroline, had been poor when Alphonse found her, the daughter of an old friend ruined by misfortune, and he had married her out of love and protection both. Victor grows up believing the world is a safe house. His parents do not rule him. They guide him. He is given books, time, and the feeling that his curiosity is a gift.

Two children arrive who will shape every later choice.

The first is Elizabeth. In the version of the story most people know, she is a beautiful orphan girl the family adopts, bright and gentle, and Victor's mother places the child's hand in his and tells him, half joking and half in earnest, that Elizabeth is a gift for him. Victor takes the sentence as a fate. From that day he thinks of Elizabeth as his to protect, his cousin in the home, and, when they are grown, his wife. She becomes the idea of goodness in his life. He does not examine that idea. He just trusts it.

The second is Henry Clerval, a school friend with none of Victor's grim intensity and all of a poet's appetite for the wide world. Henry wants languages, stories, heroism, the road. Where Victor turns inward, Henry turns outward. He is the friend Victor will fail.

A younger brother, William, is born later, adored by everyone. And there is Justine Moritz, a girl taken into the household as a servant and treated almost as a daughter, especially by Caroline. Remember Justine. The house is full of people who love Victor without condition. That is the fortune he spends.

Victor's own obsession starts innocently, the way many obsessions do. He finds a volume of old alchemical writers, Cornelius Agrippa and the others who promised that nature had a secret door. A modern man would laugh. Victor does not laugh. He is thirteen, and the old pages tell him that a disciplined mind might command the hidden forces of the world. His father glances at the book and says only that it is trash, without explaining why. The dismissal is too light. Victor hears it as ignorance, and he dives deeper.

Then grief arrives and teaches him the wrong lesson. Elizabeth falls ill with scarlet fever. Caroline nurses her and catches the disease herself. She dies. On her deathbed she joins Victor's hand with Elizabeth's again and asks them to be the support of the family she is leaving. Victor watches the person he loves most pass out of reach, and somewhere under the sorrow a question hardens. Why should life be something that only leaves. Why should a mind as strong as his have to stand and accept it.

He is seventeen when his father sends him to the university at Ingolstadt, in Germany, to study. Elizabeth stays. Henry is kept at home by his own father, who wants him in the family business and not wandering after poems. Victor leaves Geneva alone, carrying his mother's death and a hunger he has not named yet.

# The Spark

Ingolstadt disappoints him and then captures him.

A professor of natural philosophy, Monsieur Krempe, mocks the old alchemists Victor still half worships. The mockery stings. Another professor, Monsieur Waldman, is kinder and more dangerous. He tells Victor that the modern scientists have done what the alchemists only dreamed. They have opened nature. They have named her workings. They have pulled power out of the air and the metals. The old masters failed, Waldman says, but they aimed at greatness, and the new men, humble as they look, have found the real tools.

Victor walks out of that lecture a different person. He says later that he felt as if his soul were grasping at a new order of things. He stops sleeping like a normal student. He attends lectures. He builds a laboratory in his rooms. Chemistry, anatomy, the decay of bodies, the spark that distinguishes a living tissue from a dead one. He is brilliant, and brilliance with no one to answer to becomes a closed room.

For two years he pursues a single question. Where does life come from, and can it be started again after it has gone. He works with materials other people would not touch. He studies death in order to reverse it. And one night, in a flash he never fully explains, because he is afraid of handing the secret to another ambitious man, he discovers the principle. He learns how to animate lifeless matter.

The discovery should send him running to a teacher, or to Henry, or home. It sends him deeper into the room. He decides he will not begin with something small. A simple organism would be beneath the size of his ambition. He will build a human being. He will build it large, because the fine work of nerves and muscle is easier at a larger scale. He will build it from parts he gathers in charnel houses, dissecting rooms, and slaughterhouses. He tells himself he is a benefactor of the world. He imagines a new species that would bless him as its creator.

What he does not imagine is the body in front of him.

The work takes months and ruins him. He will not write to his family, because he cannot bear to pause. Summer passes. He grows thin, feverish, and frightened of his own notes, and still he will not stop. The thing on the table becomes taller than a man. The skin, stretched over assembled parts, is yellow and barely covers the muscle beneath. The hair is black and glossy. The teeth are white. The eyes, when he fits them, are watery and pale. In pieces, some of the features are beautiful. Together they are wrong. The wrongness is not one flaw. It is the feeling that a person has been made by force.

On a rainy night in November, about one in the morning, Victor finishes. He gathers the instruments of life. He sends the spark into the body.

The yellow eye opens.

The creature breathes. A convulsive motion runs through its limbs. It lives. Victor looks at what he has made, and every dream of glory collapses into disgust. He cannot stand the eye that looks back. He rushes out of the laboratory, paces his bedroom, and at last falls into a kind of sleep, the sleep of a man who has been awake too long to be sane.

He dreams of Elizabeth, walking in the streets of Ingolstadt. He dreams that he kisses her, and that the kiss turns her into the corpse of his dead mother, worms crawling in the folds of the shroud. He wakes with a scream.

The creature is there. It has followed him. It is standing by the bed, pulling the curtain aside, and it is trying, with a sound that is not yet speech, to reach him. It stretches out a hand. The hand is the hand of a child who wants its parent. Victor sees a monster. He runs. He abandons, in the first hour of its life, the being he brought into the world.

# Henry

Victor flees into the courtyard and spends the rest of the night walking the streets of Ingolstadt in the rain, terrified that the creature is behind every corner. By morning he finds himself at an inn, and there, by a mercy he has not earned, is Henry Clerval.

Henry has finally persuaded his father to let him study, and he has come to surprise his friend. He finds Victor wild-eyed, thin, talking too fast, and then talking not at all. Victor drags him back toward the rooms and makes him go up the stairs first. He cannot bear to see the creature again, and he cannot bear for Henry to see it either. They enter. The laboratory is empty. The creature is gone.

The relief breaks Victor. He collapses into a nervous fever that lasts months. Henry, who knows nothing of the laboratory except that his friend is destroying himself, nurses him without asking the questions Victor will not survive. He writes cheerful letters home so the family in Geneva will not panic. He reads to Victor. He walks him back toward ordinary daylight. By spring Victor can speak of lakes and mountains again, and he can almost pretend the night in November was a sickness of the mind.

A letter from Elizabeth pulls the real world back. She writes with the old affection, gives him news of his father, of little William, of Justine. She asks, gently, whether his studies have made him love another woman, and she releases him if they have. She also reminds him that he promised to come home. Victor reads the letter and weeps, and he writes back that he is theirs, that he is coming, that science has cost him enough.

He delays even so. He and Henry study languages together, Persian, Arabic, the poetry Henry loves. Victor is happy in a thin way. He has decided, privately, that he will never again pursue the principle he discovered. He thinks the story is over because he has chosen to stop telling it.

Then a letter comes from his father. William, the little boy, has been murdered.

# William and Justine

The letter is simple and unbearable. The family had been walking near Lake Geneva, at Plainpalais. William ran ahead and did not return. They searched through the night. In the morning a peasant woman found the child. Marks on his neck showed he had been strangled. A miniature portrait of Caroline, which William had been wearing in a locket, was gone.

Alphonse begs Victor to come home and help them bear it.

Victor travels day and night. He reaches Geneva after dark, the city gates already shut, and he waits in the rain at the spot where William died. Thunder moves over the Jura mountains. Lightning shows him the lake, the trees, and then, in a flash, a figure between the trees. It is huge. It is the shape he ran from in Ingolstadt. For one second the lightning gives him the yellow face, and then the dark takes it again.

Victor understands at once, with a certainty no court would accept. The creature killed his brother. The being he abandoned has found his family.

He cannot say this to anyone. Who would believe that a student built a man. He goes home, and the house gives him a second horror. Justine, the young woman who loved William like a sister, has been accused of the murder. The locket was found in her pocket. She had been out all night. She cannot explain the portrait. She swears she is innocent. Elizabeth believes her. Victor believes her too, and he knows who is guilty, and he says nothing that can save her.

The trial is a study in how a good person's silence becomes a weapon. Justine has a gentle character, a history of kindness, and no motive anyone can make convincing. She also has a piece of evidence she cannot answer, and a public that wants the death of a child to have a human cause. Her confessor presses her until, exhausted and terrified of hell, she confesses to a crime she did not commit, hoping God at least will know the truth. Elizabeth speaks for her in court, bravely, and it is not enough.

Justine is hanged.

Victor walks through the days after like a man in a locked room. His father, who does not know the real story, tries to turn him toward endurance and faith. Elizabeth's brightness begins to die. She says, in so many words, that she has lost her faith in human goodness. Victor hears this and knows he is the cause of it, twice. Once by what he made. Once by what he would not tell.

He starts to avoid the people he loves because their faces accuse him. He takes a boat out alone. He rows until the lake is a dark plate and the mountains are only a line. He thinks of drowning. The water offers him an ending, and he refuses it, not from courage, but because he has begun to suspect the creature is not finished with them.

# The Meeting on the Ice

Seeking air and punishment, Victor travels into the valley of Chamonix. The glaciers calm other travelers. They do not calm him. He climbs toward Montanvert, over the Mer de Glace, the sea of ice, with a guide, and then he goes on alone because he cannot bear company. The world up there is broken rock and white light. It suits the inside of his head.

The creature finds him there.

Victor sees it bounding over the crevasses with a speed no ordinary man could manage. It has grown into its strength. The cold does not stop it. Victor trembles with rage and hatred and, under those, a fear he hates even more. The creature approaches and speaks.

This is the turn of the book. The thing Victor has called a monster has a voice, and the voice is educated, sorrowful, and exact.

It tells Victor to come with it to a hut on the mountain, out of the weather, and to hear its story before he condemns it again. Victor refuses, curses it, and tries to attack it. The creature holds him off without effort and without striking back. It says a sentence Victor cannot answer. It says that it was benevolent and good, and that misery made it a fiend. It says Victor is its creator and owes it. It says that if Victor will listen, and then grant one request, it will leave humankind alone forever. If he refuses, it will work at his ruin until his life is ash.

Victor goes to the hut. He listens. For the length of the creature's tale, the novel changes hands.

# The Creature's Education

The creature's first hours are a nightmare of sensation without names. Light hurts. Dark frightens. Cold bites. Hunger is a pain with no explanation. It stumbles out of Victor's rooms and into a forest near Ingolstadt, naked, bewildered, and already learning that the world strikes.

It discovers fire because a traveler has left a camp. Fire warms, and fire burns. That double lesson, that a good thing can injure you if you come too close, is the pattern of everything that follows. It eats berries, roots, and stolen scraps. It drinks from streams. It learns, by being driven off with stones, that human beings hate the sight of it.

Wandering, it finds a small hovel built against the back wall of a cottage. The hovel is miserable, but it has a crack in the wall, and through the crack the creature can see a family. It stays. Watching them becomes its life.

The family is poor, foreign, and gentle with one another. There is a blind old father, De Lacey. There is a young man, Felix, and a young woman, Agatha. They have very little to eat. The creature, hiding in the hovel, notices that he has been stealing from their small store of food, and the guilt of it, once he understands what hunger means for them, changes him. He stops taking their meals. He begins, at night, to cut firewood and leave it at their door, anonymous, so their labor will be lighter. He cries when they play music. He does not yet know the word for crying.

He learns language the long way, by attention. Felix is teaching someone to speak his tongue. A visitor arrives, a young woman named Safie, and the cottage changes key. Safie is the daughter of a Turkish merchant Felix once helped. She loves Felix. She has fled a father who would have betrayed the family that saved him. She does not speak the language well, so Felix teaches her, slowly, with smiles, and the creature on the other side of the wall studies every lesson. Words attach to things. Things attach to feelings. Grammar arrives. Then history.

From their readings he learns what human beings are. He learns of empires, cruelty, kindness, the division of property, the fact that some people are born into warmth and some are born outside the door. He learns that he has no papers, no mother, no place in any of those stories. A book of Volney's ruins of empires, read aloud in the cottage, gives him the brutality of the species. It also gives him the idea of justice, which is worse, because justice implies he was owed something.

In the forest one day he finds a leather case with three books and some papers. The books are Paradise Lost, Plutarch's Lives, and The Sorrows of Werter. He reads them as if they were maps of his own condition. Plutarch gives him men to admire. Werter gives him a private storm of feeling. Paradise Lost gives him Adam, who awoke, as he did, into a world already made, and who had a God who answered when he spoke. The creature compares himself to Adam and then, more bitterly, to Satan, because Adam was loved and he is not. The papers in the case are worse than the books. They are Victor's laboratory journal. From them the creature learns his own origin. He learns the disgust of the man who made him. He learns the word creator, and he learns that his creator ran.

He has been watching the De Laceys for nearly a year. He has one hope left, and it is a careful one. The old man is blind. If he enters when Felix, Agatha, and Safie are away, De Lacey may judge him by his voice and his kindness, not by his face. He practices speeches. He waits for the right hour. He knocks.

The conversation, for a few minutes, is the gentlest scene in the novel. De Lacey hears a stranger in trouble and offers the only thing he has, which is attention. The creature begins to tell him that he seeks the protection of friends who have never seen him. The old man is moved. He says that the hearts of men, when unprejudiced by self-interest, are full of brotherly love. It is the sentence the creature has been living for.

Then Felix, Agatha, and Safie return. Agatha faints. Safie runs. Felix, seeing a giant crouched at his blind father's feet, tears the creature away and beats him with a stick. The creature could kill Felix with one hand. It does not. It flees back to the hovel, and something in it, the part that believed in brotherly love, begins to rot.

# The Demand

The creature stays nearby, unseen, and watches the family decide they are no longer safe. They leave the cottage. They do not know they are abandoning the only being who has loved them in secret. The creature returns, finds the rooms empty, and for the first time chooses destruction on purpose. He sets the cottage on fire and leaves it burning. The anonymous woodcutter is gone. What walks away from that fire is someone who has decided the world will not love him, and who intends to answer in kind.

He travels toward Geneva because the journal named the city and the man. The journey takes months. He keeps to the woods and the ice. Once, in a wood, he sees a young girl fall into a stream and begin to drown. He saves her. A man, the girl's companion, sees the rescue, sees the face, and shoots him. The bullet tears his flesh. He survives, and he records the lesson. A good act, offered by him, is answered with a gun.

By the time he reaches the country outside Geneva he hates Victor with a focused, almost legal hatred. He wants recognition. He wants an answer. Near the lake he meets a beautiful child, William, and he thinks, for one last time, that a child might be too young to have learned disgust. He seizes the boy, meaning only to take him as a companion, someone who could grow up without horror. William screams that his father is Monsieur Frankenstein, that his family will punish the attacker. The name Frankenstein detonates what is left of the creature's restraint. He strangles the child. He sees the locket, the portrait of a lovely woman, and he feels, looking at it, the full measure of what he will never be given. Then he finds a barn, and a sleeping young woman, Justine, and he slips the locket into her dress. He says, later, with a terrible calm, that he is malicious because he is miserable. He has learned how to make humans destroy each other.

This is the story he tells Victor on the glacier. He does not ask forgiveness first. He asks for a condition.

He is alone, and alone is not a life. Every other creature he has seen has a mate. He wants one. He wants Victor to build him a female, as hideous as himself if need be, so that they can leave Europe, go to the wilds of South America, and never be seen by humankind again. If Victor refuses, he will murder everyone Victor loves, and he will make the work last.

Victor is revolted. He argues. He calls the creature a devil. The creature answers, point by point, and the answers are the best reasoning in the book. He did not ask to be made. He was made, and then he was left. His violence began after the world taught him that kindness was useless. Give him justice, he says, and he will be harmless. Deny him, and he will have nothing left but revenge, which at least is a purpose.

Victor, exhausted, horrified, and half convinced, agrees. He will build a companion. The creature swears that on the day he receives her, Victor will never see either of them again. Then he vanishes across the ice, and Victor is left with a promise that feels like a second fall.

# The Second Creature

Victor goes home and cannot do the work there. Elizabeth's eyes, his father's trust, the graves of William and Justine, all of it makes the laboratory impossible in Geneva. He tells his family he must travel to England for study, a lie with a true destination inside it. His father, still hoping marriage will settle him, asks that he and Elizabeth marry on his return. Victor agrees, and privately he hears the creature's old threat. I will be with you on your wedding night. He thinks the threat is aimed at his own life. He is wrong about the target, and the mistake will cost everything.

Henry Clerval comes with him. Henry's joy in the journey, the Rhine, the universities, the talk of future explorations in India, is almost cruel to read against Victor's silence. Victor loves him and cannot tell him why every lovely day feels like a countdown. They reach London. Victor gathers the instruments and the knowledge he needs, hating every preparation. Then, seeking a place lonely enough for the crime of building another being, he leaves Henry in Scotland and goes alone to a remote island in the Orkneys, a scrap of rock with a few miserable huts and a population that asks no questions of a foreign scholar.

He sets up a laboratory. He begins the female.

The work is worse than the first time because he no longer has the anesthesia of ambition. He knows what the yellow eye means. He works anyway, because he is afraid of the creature's revenge, and because a promise, even a monstrous one, still has a hold on him. Weeks pass. The figure on the table grows. One evening he looks up and sees, at the window of the hut, the creature watching him. It has followed him across Europe. It has come to collect.

In that look Victor's nerve breaks in a new direction. He thinks of what he is about to release. A female who might refuse the male. A female who might be ten times more malignant. Children. A race. He thinks of the promise made on a glacier under duress, and he decides that his duty to the human world is larger than his bargain with one sufferer. He tears the half-finished creature apart with his hands.

The watcher at the window sees it. The howl it gives is despair first and fury second. Later that night it comes into the hut and speaks to Victor with a control that is more frightening than the howl. It reminds him of the promise. It tells him he may destroy the work, but he cannot destroy the bond. Victor is its creator. It will not be abandoned a second time without an answer. Then it says the line that governs the rest of the novel. It will be with him on his wedding night.

It leaves. Victor is certain, now, that he himself will die on that night. He gathers the remains of the female into a basket, takes a boat out onto the sea, and sinks them with stones. The wind rises. He loses the shore. He drifts, half dead, and is blown at last to the Irish coast.

He is not received as a shipwrecked scholar. He is received as a murderer. A body has washed up, a young man strangled, with marks the villagers have already learned to fear. They drag Victor in and show him the corpse. It is Henry Clerval.

The sight finishes what the fever in Ingolstadt started. Victor collapses into raving illness. He is nursed in prison. For a long time he is not sane enough to stand trial. When he is, the evidence cannot hold him. He was at sea when Henry died. A kindly magistrate sees a broken foreigner rather than a killer. Victor is acquitted, and his father, who has crossed Europe to reach him, takes him home. Alphonse's hair has gone white. He does not understand what his son is carrying. He only knows that two sons, in effect, are already gone, because Henry was a son of the house, and Victor has come back hollow.

# The Wedding Night

They return toward Geneva slowly, because Victor cannot bear speed, and because every mile is a mile closer to the night the creature promised. Elizabeth writes. Her letter is one of the most painful things in the book, because it is brave and because it is mistaken in a merciful way. She thinks Victor's misery is doubt about the marriage. She thinks he may love another woman, or that he marries her from duty. She offers to release him. She would rather lose him than trap him.

Victor reads this and feels the full cruelty of his secrecy. He cannot tell her about the creature without sounding mad, and he will not put the promise on her shoulders. He writes back that he has a terrible secret, that he will tell her the day after they are married, and that until that day she must trust him. He believes he is arranging his own death and sparing her the sight of it. He marries her in the family hope that joy can be decided.

They spend the wedding day at a family house on the lake, planning to sleep there before continuing. The evening is beautiful in the way the book keeps offering beauty just before a blow. Wind on the water. Elizabeth's hand in his. Victor, smiling when he can, is searching every shadow for the shape that will come for him. He checks the rooms. He sends Elizabeth to bed while he walks the house with a pistol, certain the attack will fall on his body.

He hears her scream.

The scream is short. By the time he reaches the room, Elizabeth is dead across the bed, strangled, her face the picture his mother became in the nightmare at Ingolstadt. The window is open. Out on the lake, in the moonlight, the creature is visible for a moment, and it gestures toward the corpse with a triumph that is also, if you have heard its story, a hideous kind of grief. Victor fires. The creature dives and is gone. The wedding night has been kept, and Victor has understood it too late. The creature did not mean to kill the maker. It meant to make the maker as alone as itself.

Alphonse, brought to this last body, cannot survive it. He dies of the shock in Victor's arms. The family that opened the book, Caroline, William, Justine, Henry, Elizabeth, the father, is gone. Victor tells a magistrate everything. The magistrate believes he is mad, or believes him and still refuses to chase a being that can cross glaciers. The law has no tool for this. Victor swears the revenge himself. He will follow the creature until one of them is dead.

# The Hunt

The last movement of Victor's story is a pursuit that inverts the first crime. Once the creature followed a maker who would not look at it. Now the maker follows the creature across the earth, and the creature will not let him stop.

Victor tracks it through the Mediterranean countries, up into Russia, into the frozen north. The creature leaves messages, sometimes in bark, sometimes carved, sometimes spoken to people Victor questions. The messages are goads. They call him onward. They taunt his exhaustion. They also, if you listen, sound like a being that still wants to be chased, because the chase is the only relationship it has left. Come, it says. I am afraid of nothing except that you will tire before the work is done.

Dogs die. Sledges break. Victor's health, never restored, thins to a thread. He reaches the ice where Walton's ship is trapped, and there, within sight of his enemy, he collapses. The crew of the ship saves him. The creature, still ahead on the ice, passes out of reach again. Victor has told the whole story to Walton by the time his strength fails for the last time.

Here the frame closes, and Walton becomes the witness.

The ship is in danger. The ice may crush it. The sailors, who did not swear Victor's oath, demand that Walton turn south if a passage opens. Walton hesitates. He has the ambition Victor warned him about, and he has just spent weeks listening to the bill for that ambition. Victor, dying, gathers himself for one last blaze and tells the crew they will be dishonored if they retreat. He calls them cowards. Then the blaze goes out. He admits, more quietly, to Walton, that he had no right to ask them to die for his revenge. He has learned the lesson and learned it too late to save anyone he loved. He still cannot quite repent of the pursuit of knowledge itself. He pities his creature and condemns it. He asks Walton, if the creature appears, to destroy it. Then he dies, believing, to the last, that another man might have completed his science without his ruin. It is the most Victor sentence in the book. Even at the end he half imagines a version of himself that did not fail.

Walton mourns him as the friend he wanted. The passage south opens. He agrees to turn the ship around. The ambition cools into grief.

That night, or in the hour when the ship is quiet, Walton hears a sound in the cabin where Victor's body lies. He goes in. The creature is standing over the coffin.

# The Creature's Last Words

Walton's first impulse is the world's impulse. Horror, then the wish to attack. The creature stops him with grief that cannot be performed. It bends over Victor. It speaks to the dead man, not to the captain. It says that it begged for pity and was answered with hatred, and that hatred has eaten everything, including the satisfaction of revenge. Killing Elizabeth, driving Alphonse to death, hunting Victor to this bed, none of it has given the creature a life. It has only made a mirror of misery, two beings bound together, both destroyed.

Walton accuses it. He has heard Victor's version, and he is loyal to the dead friend. The creature does not deny the murders. It weeps, and the weeping is not a trick, or Shelley would not have given it the last and best speech in the novel. It says it was full of the capacity for love. It says virtue once felt possible, in the days of the blind old man and the firewood left at a door. It says the change into a fiend was real, and that the change is its own misery now, because a being who can remember goodness cannot enjoy evil in peace. Remorse is the one human inheritance it successfully received.

It tells Walton what it will do. It will leave the ship. It will go farther north, onto the ice, build a funeral pyre, and burn itself until the body Victor assembled is ash and the secret of its making dies with it. It will not give humankind another chance to hate it, and it will not give another scientist a specimen. Its death is the only restitution it can still make, and it knows restitution is not the same as forgiveness. No one left alive can forgive it. Elizabeth cannot. Henry cannot. William cannot. Victor, who might have shared the blame, is a body in a box.

The creature springs from the cabin window onto the raft of ice beside the ship. The current takes it. Walton watches the figure grow small, lost in darkness and distance, and Shelley's book ends there, on a being that is still, for a moment, alive, and already beyond pursuit.

# What the Story Leaves Behind

Frankenstein is remembered as a story about a monster. Heard all the way through, it is a story about a refusal to care for what you make.

Victor wants the glory of creation and none of the daily work of love. He wants a triumph, not a child. The creature, for its first long stretch of life, wants exactly what the De Laceys already have: a language, a task, someone who will sit still while it speaks. The murders are real. They are not excused by the listening. They are explained by it, which is a harder and more useful thing. Justine dies because a locket was planted and because a good man would not speak. Elizabeth dies because a threat was heard and aimed at the wrong person. Henry dies because he stood next to a secret. The ice at the end is not an adventure. It is what remains when ambition has used up every warm room it was given.

Captain Walton sails home. He has been offered the same dare Victor accepted in Ingolstadt, the dare to be extraordinary at any cost, and he has been shown the invoice. The novel's last kindness is that he turns the ship around.

This is the end of the story of Frankenstein.
