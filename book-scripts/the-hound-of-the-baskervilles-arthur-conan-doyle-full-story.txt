# Introduction

Welcome to Mighty Chapters. This is the complete story of The Hound of the Baskervilles, the novel Arthur Conan Doyle published in nineteen oh-two.

The book is in the public domain. This is an original retelling, in new words, made for listening. The legend, the warning, the moor, the man on the tor, and the last run of the hound are all here. Nothing that solves the case is skipped.

Doctor Watson tells it. He is Sherlock Holmes's friend, the man who writes the cases down, and for a long stretch of this one he believes he is working alone. He is not a fool. He is a brave, decent observer who cannot see around a corner that Holmes has chosen to keep dark.

# The Legend

Holmes and Watson are at breakfast in Baker Street when a visitor leaves a walking stick in the hall and steps out again, long enough for Holmes to read the stick the way other people read a letter. The owner is a country doctor, late middle age, with a dog, a practice given to him by a grateful patient, and a careful, kindly nature. The visitor returns and is astonished. His name is Doctor James Mortimer. The stick was a gift. The dog is real. He did not come to be amazed. He came because he is frightened.

He has two things in his pocket. A manuscript, and the newspaper account of a death.

The manuscript is old, dated seventeen forty-two, and it tells the legend of the Baskervilles. Hugo Baskerville, a coarse and cruel man, kidnapped a young woman on the Devon moor and locked her in an upper room. She escaped. He chased her across the moor with his hounds and his drunken friends, swearing he would give himself to the powers of evil if he could catch her. The friends found her dead of fear and exhaustion, and found Hugo dead beside her, and a great black hound, larger than any normal dog, tearing out his throat. The curse, the manuscript says, has hung over the family ever since. No Baskerville should cross the moor in the hours of darkness, when the hound is abroad.

Mortimer does not enjoy legends. He enjoys facts, and the facts have started to rhyme with the story. Sir Charles Baskerville, the last owner of Baskerville Hall, was an elderly man with a weak heart and a generous reputation. He believed the legend enough to be nervous at night, and he walked anyway, for his health, down a yew alley on his estate. He was found dead at the end of the alley, no mark on him, his face seized by terror. Near the body, on the soft ground at the edge of the moor, were the footprints of a gigantic hound.

There is an heir. Sir Henry Baskerville, young, raised partly in Canada, is coming to London to claim the estate. Mortimer wants Holmes to tell him whether Sir Henry should go home to Devon or stay away. Holmes listens, asks for time, and looks at a map of the moor as if the legend were a geography problem, which, to him, it is.

Sir Henry arrives with a concrete problem of his own. A letter has reached him at his hotel, made of words cut from a newspaper and pasted on paper. It warns him to stay away from the moor if he values his life. The letters were cut with nail scissors, from a leading article in yesterday's Times, which means the sender was in London, working in a hurry, with a good newspaper and a poor plan. One of Sir Henry's boots is stolen from the hotel, then returned, then another boot, a newer one, vanishes. A bearded man follows him in a cab and shouts a wrong address when he is spotted. Holmes sends the boy Cartwright to check the wastepaper of the hotels, and he sends a telegram, and he thinks. He is not ready to leave London. He tells Watson, to Watson's surprise and slight hurt, that Watson will go to Devon with Sir Henry. Holmes has work in town. Watson is to watch, to protect, and to report everything. Who meets Sir Henry. Who walks on the moor. Who is afraid.

Watson accepts. He usually does. The praise Holmes gives him at the door, that he is the man he would choose at his side when there is danger, carries him onto the train.

# Baskerville Hall

Devon is beautiful in the way that does not comfort. Green lanes, then the long brown roll of the moor, tors of rock, and Baskerville Hall itself, an old house with twin towers, a door black with age, and a sense of having watched foolish men arrive before. Barrymore, the butler, and his wife run the house. They inherited their posts from Sir Charles's time. They are correct, pale, and already living with their nerves. Sir Henry tries to be cheerful and practical. He wants to be a good landlord. He talks of electric light and repairs. The moor does not care about his plans.

Watson writes to Holmes and describes the neighbors, because Holmes has asked for the whole cast.

There is Stapleton, a naturalist, thin and eager, who lives at Merripit House with his sister, a dark, beautiful woman named Beryl. Stapleton knows the moor, the butterflies, the dangerous mire called the Grimpen Mire, where a pony can wander and vanish in the bog. He is friendly in a quick, intense way. Beryl is friendly and then, the moment her brother is out of hearing, frightened. She mistakes Watson for Sir Henry at one of their first meetings and warns him, urgently, to go back to London. When she learns he is only a friend, the warning snaps shut. Stapleton returns, and she becomes a hostess again. Watson files the moment. He does not yet know what to do with it.

There is Frankland, an elderly neighbor who lives for lawsuits and for watching the moor through a telescope, a foolish man who will accidentally supply a useful fact. There are the Barrymores, whose faces at the windows bother Watson at night. And there is an escaped convict on the moor, Selden, the Notting Hill murderer, said to be hiding among the huts and the old stone ruins. A dangerous man in a dangerous place gives the local fear a body. Not every scream in the dark will be a legend. Some of them may be a killer in prison clothes.

Sir Henry is drawn to Beryl Stapleton. Stapleton does not like it. He comes to Watson in a heat that seems too strong for a brother's ordinary caution, and he warns Sir Henry off with a violence that startles everyone, then apologizes the next day and explains it away as the loneliness of a man who cannot bear to lose his sister's company. Sir Henry, who is straightforward, accepts the apology more easily than Watson does. Watson writes it all down for Holmes. The replies he receives are short. Holmes is deep in a case in London. Keep watching.

At night Watson cannot sleep. He hears a woman crying in the house. Barrymore denies it in the morning with a face that makes the denial useless. Then, one night, Watson and Sir Henry see Barrymore at a window with a candle, signaling toward the moor. They confront him. Mrs. Barrymore breaks. The convict Selden is her brother. The candle is a signal that food has been left, a sister's help, not a plot against Sir Henry. The two men go out onto the moor to try to catch Selden anyway, because pity for a sister is not the same as safety in a house. They hear the convict running. They also hear, far out across the rocks, a sound that is not a man. A howl, long, low, and alive. Sir Henry, who has been brave about legends, grips Watson's arm. Even an escaped murderer does not explain that sound.

On the way back, Watson sees a figure standing on a tor, a tall thin man against the moon, not Selden, not Barrymore, not anyone who should be there. The figure vanishes. Watson has a new fact and no box to put it in. Someone else is on the moor, watching, as he is watching.

# The Man on the Tor

The case tightens through ordinary conversations, which is how Holmes cases tighten.

Watson meets Laura Lyons, the daughter of Frankland, a woman living in a nearby town, estranged from her father, separated from a husband, trying to earn a living by typewriting. She had written to Sir Charles asking for a meeting on the very day he died. She will not, at first, say why. Money, a desperate letter, a promise of help. She did not keep the appointment. Someone else persuaded her not to go. She will not name that person yet. Watson leaves knowing Sir Charles went to the gate that night expecting a woman who never came, and that someone used her letter as a way to put the old man where they wanted him.

Barrymore, grateful that his wife's secret has been treated with some mercy, offers a secret in return. Sir Charles received a letter that burned in the grate, a letter asking him to be at the gate, and the handwriting was a woman's. The burned scrap matches what Laura has admitted. The hound's prints remain. The human plot and the monstrous print are starting to occupy the same night.

Watson decides he must find the man on the tor. Frankland, the lawsuit neighbor, has seen a boy carrying food to the old stone huts through his telescope, and he thinks he has found the convict. Watson knows the convict is fed by the Barrymores. A second supply line means a second hidden man. He climbs to the hut alone.

The hut is empty and recently lived in. Blankets. Ashes. A note that says Watson has gone to Coombe Tracey. The watcher knows his movements. Watson waits with his revolver out, feeling, for once, like the hunter. Footsteps come up the path. He cocks the pistol. Sherlock Holmes walks in.

The hurt comes first, and Holmes expects it. He has been on the moor the whole time, in the hut, reading Watson's reports at closer range than London, letting Watson believe he was alone so that the household at the Hall would behave naturally. He praises the reports. He has been cruel, and he has been effective, and Watson, after the anger, is too glad to see him to keep the anger lit. Holmes has not been idle. He knows about Laura Lyons. He knows about the candle. He knows the howl. And he knows the thing Watson has misread from the beginning. Beryl Stapleton is not Stapleton's sister. She is his wife. The warning she tried to give was a wife's warning, given against a husband's plan. Stapleton has been playing the neighbor while he laid a trap.

They are not given a quiet evening to enjoy the reunion. A scream rises on the moor, and the hound's cry rises with it. They run. They find a body that has fallen from a height, smashed on the rocks, wearing Sir Henry's clothes. Watson is sick with the thought that they have protected a man into his death. Holmes bends over the face and cries out with relief. It is Selden, the convict. Sir Henry had given his old clothes to Barrymore, and Barrymore had given them to his brother-in-law. The hound was set on a scent. It followed the scent of the heir, and it killed the wrong man. Somewhere in the dark, Stapleton has called his animal off a corpse that does not profit him.

They hear steps. Stapleton arrives, composed, too late to stop them seeing the body, and for a moment his disappointment shows before the concern covers it. He explains he came out because of the sound. He looks at the dead convict and offers the moor’s dangers as a complete explanation. Holmes watches him with a face Watson knows, the face of a man who has the shape of a case and is waiting for the last move that will make it provable. They do not arrest him. A dead convict and a howl in the dark will not hold in a courtroom. They need the hound itself, and they need Sir Henry alive as the bait Stapleton cannot resist.

Holmes warns Sir Henry, without telling him everything, to accept an invitation to dine at Merripit House, and then to walk home across the moor. It is a hard thing to ask. Sir Henry, who came to Devon to take up an honest life and has met a woman he cares for, agrees, because Holmes asks it and because he is tired of being the object of a mystery. Watson understands that they are using their friend as a lure. Holmes understands it too and does it anyway, with the calculation that a controlled risk is safer than the uncontrolled one Sir Henry is already living inside.

# The Hound

The night of the dinner is fog and rock.

Holmes, Watson, and a detective from London, Lestrade, whom Holmes has summoned because he wants an official witness, hide outside Merripit House. Through the window they see Sir Henry at the table with Stapleton. Beryl is not there. A fog rolls in from the mire, thicker than Holmes wanted, eating the path. If it covers the ground between the house and their hiding place, Sir Henry will be walking blind, and so will they.

Stapleton leaves the room. Minutes pass. Sir Henry comes out alone and starts for home along the path they told him to trust. The fog is at his heels. Then, out of the fog, the hound comes.

Watson has heard the howl and has tried to be rational. The sight of the animal ends the argument. It is huge. It is black. Its jaws and eyes burn with a fire that looks, in the first second, like hell, and in the second second like a chemical light painted onto a living dog. It runs with a speed that turns Sir Henry into prey. Holmes and Watson and Lestrade fire. The hound yelps, staggers, and still leaps. It takes Sir Henry down. Holmes empties his revolver into it at the closest range and kills it. The fire dies on the muzzle as the animal dies. Phosphorus. A mixture, cruel and clever, wiped on the mouth and eyes of an enormous dog to make a legend visible. Under the glow it is a dog, mortal, and dead.

Sir Henry is alive, shaken to the edge of his nerves, the throat torn at by the cloth of his jacket more than by the teeth. They leave him and go to the house, because Beryl is missing and because Stapleton, hearing the shots, has run.

They find Beryl bound in an upstairs room, gagged, the bruises of a long coercion on her. When she understands the hound is dead and her husband is the quarry, she gives them the last piece. Stapleton has a hiding place in the heart of the Grimpen Mire, a path he has marked with sticks, a hut where he kept the hound. The fog and the dark make the sticks a poor promise. They follow as far as they can. They hear a man ahead, and then they hear nothing, and then the mire has him. Stapleton does not come out. The bog takes the cleverest criminal Holmes has met, which is a colder sentence than a rope, and a less satisfying one. There is no body to show a jury. There is a widow, a terrified heir, and a dead dog with paint on its face.

# The Explanation

Back in Baker Street, later, Holmes lays the case out for Watson the way a man puts tools back in a box.

Stapleton was a Baskerville. Not the heir, the hidden branch. The family history contains a younger son, Rodger Baskerville, who went to South America, was said to have died unmarried, and in fact had a son. That son, clever, poor relative to the fortune, and patient, came back under a new name. He married Beryl in circumstances that gave him power over her. He set himself up beside the estate as a naturalist, learned the mire, and bought or stole a hound large enough to terrify. He did not need magic. He needed a heart condition, a legend everyone already believed, and a scent.

Sir Charles was the first death. Laura Lyons, desperate for money to free herself from her husband, was encouraged by Stapleton, who had cultivated her, to write and ask Sir Charles for help, and to ask him to meet her at the gate at night. Stapleton then persuaded her not to go. Sir Charles went. He waited in the dark, an old man with a weak heart and a family story about a hound. Stapleton released the painted animal. The old man ran, and his heart stopped, and the footprints at the edge of the path did the rest of the work. No wound was required. Fear, aimed precisely, was enough. The letter burned in the grate. The woman who had written it stayed silent because she had been used and because telling the truth would ruin her.

Sir Henry's arrival restarted the plan. The warning letter, cut from the Times, was Beryl's attempt to save a stranger without quite betraying a husband. The stolen boot was for the scent, and the first boot, a new one, was useless because Sir Henry had not worn it enough. The second boot worked. Selden died in Sir Henry's clothes because a hound follows a smell, not a face. The man on the tor was Holmes. The sister was a wife. The hellfire was phosphorus, kept in a tin, wiped on a dog that was hungry and trained and, in its own way, another victim.

Holmes is not proud of the mire. He would have preferred a dock and a witness and a sentence. He is satisfied that Sir Henry is alive and that the legend has been returned to what it always was, a story used by a modern man as cover. Sir Henry's nerves are badly hurt. He goes on a long voyage with Doctor Mortimer to recover, away from yew alleys and fog. Baskerville Hall stays standing, emptier than before. Beryl is free and damaged. Watson writes it down. The hound, which London will talk about as if it were still running, is a body in a shed, mortal once the light on it is allowed to go out.

# What the Story Leaves Behind

The Hound of the Baskervilles works because the supernatural explanation is allowed to feel true for as long as the evidence supports it. The prints are real. The howl is real. The fire on the jaws is real. Holmes's gift is not that he refuses wonder. It is that he refuses to stop at wonder. He asks who benefits, who can cross the mire, who wrote the letter, whose wife is being called a sister.

Watson carries the middle of the book because courage without the full picture is its own kind of story. He watches, he protects a man he likes, he climbs to a hut with a revolver, and he is angry, rightly, when he learns he was a piece on the board. Holmes put him there because Stapleton would have hidden the truth if the great detective had arrived at the Hall in daylight. The method is cold. The result is a living heir.

Under the case there is a simpler cruelty. A poor branch of a family looks at a rich branch and decides the difference is a death or two. Stapleton is intelligent enough to make a legend do the killing. He is not intelligent enough to see that a wife, a neighbor with a telescope, a butler with a guilty conscience, and a doctor who writes everything down are all ways a secret leaks. The mire closes over him. The moor stays. The ordinary dog, washed of its fire, is the answer, and the answer is worse than the legend, because a person built it.

This is the end of the story of The Hound of the Baskervilles.
