# Introduction

Welcome to Mighty Chapters. This is the complete story of Dracula, the novel Bram Stoker published in eighteen ninety-seven.

The book is in the public domain. The telling you are about to hear is original. The words are new, written to be spoken, and the plot is whole. Jonathan Harker's journey, Lucy's illness, the return of the dead, Mina's danger, and the race back to the castle are all here, in the order the book gives them.

Dracula is told as a bundle of documents. Diaries, letters, a ship's log, a newspaper clipping. Each person writes down what they saw, and none of them, at first, sees the whole. This retelling keeps those voices by naming, as we go, whose eyes we are looking through.

# Jonathan Goes East

Jonathan Harker is a young English solicitor, newly qualified, and he is proud of the errand that takes him out of Exeter. His firm is helping a foreign nobleman buy a house in England. The client is Count Dracula, of a castle in the Carpathian Mountains, beyond Bistritz, in a corner of Transylvania that the modern timetable barely reaches. Jonathan keeps a journal for Mina Murray, the woman he intends to marry, so she can share the trip. The journal becomes the record of his education.

The journey east is cheerful at first. Good trains, strange food, a landscape that grows older as the stations grow poorer. At Bistritz he stays at a hotel the Count has named. The landlord and his wife turn pale when they hear the destination. They do not explain much. They press on him a crucifix, which Jonathan, a modern Englishman, finds a little embarrassing and accepts to be kind. An old woman begs him not to leave that night. She talks of the eve of Saint George's Day, when, she says, evil things in the world have full sway. He smiles, thanks her, and goes.

A carriage takes him into the Borgo Pass as the light fails. It is met, exactly at the appointed hour, by another carriage, driven by a tall man with a great hat, red eyes in the lamplight, and astonishing strength. The driver loads Jonathan's trunk as if it were a parcel. Wolves begin to run beside the wheels. When they close in, the driver stands, throws out his arms, and the wolves fall back as if a command has passed over them. Jonathan sees it and files it under fear and foreign roads. He is still trying to be a solicitor.

They arrive at a ruined courtyard, a huge door, and a courtyard silence with no servants in it. The driver vanishes. The door opens before Jonathan can knock twice. Count Dracula is there, an old man in black, with a white moustache, sharp teeth under a courteous smile, and a hand as cold as the hand of a dead man. He insists on carrying the bags himself. He will not let Jonathan touch them. Inside, the castle is a museum of old power. No mirrors. No household. A table set for one.

Over supper the Count talks beautifully about the history of his people, the wars, the pride of a boyar who will not be ruled. He asks practical questions too, endless questions, about England. Trains. Legal habits. How a foreign gentleman might hire servants, move cargo, pass unnoticed in London. Jonathan, flattered to be useful, answers everything. He does not yet hear what the questions are building.

# The Castle

The first nights are strange in ways Jonathan keeps trying to rationalize. He cuts himself shaving. There is no mirror in the room, so he has hung a small travel mirror by the window. The Count enters, sees the blood on Jonathan's throat, and lunges with a sudden, animal focus. The crucifix at Jonathan's neck stops him. A moment later the Count is a host again, warning his guest that mirrors are not to be trusted in an old house, and he throws the shaving glass out of the window. Jonathan looks at the Count standing beside him and realizes the glass had held no second figure. The man cast no reflection.

Doors that were open are locked. Jonathan explores and finds that the castle is a prison with one willing inmate, and that inmate is not him. The Count is never seen in daylight. He crawls, Jonathan watches this from a window and nearly loses his mind, face-down along the castle wall like a lizard, hundreds of feet above the stones, his cloak spreading as he goes. He has the key to every door. There are no servants because he does not need them. He is the driver. He is the porter. He is the nobleman. He has been playing a household.

The worst room is a ruined chapel, or something that was once holy and has been used for a different purpose. In it Jonathan finds great wooden boxes of earth, and in one of the boxes, in the late day, he finds the Count. Dracula looks younger than the old man who greeted him. His hair is darker. His mouth is red. Blood is at the corners of it. Jonathan understands, with a solicitor's horror of an unsigned fact, that he is looking at a body that is not alive and not at rest.

Before that discovery, there is the night of the three women.

Jonathan disobeys a warning and sleeps in a room that is not his own. Three women appear, beautiful, pale, with bright hard eyes and teeth that spoil the beauty when they smile. They bend over him. One is about to put her mouth to his throat. He is paralyzed, half in terror and half in a dreadful willingness. Then the Count arrives in fury, seizes the woman, and hurls her back. He tells them this man belongs to him until he is finished with him. He promises them that when he is done, they may have Jonathan. He gives them, to quiet them, a bag that moves and cries. Jonathan faints. He will spend the rest of his life wishing he had not understood what was in the bag.

From that night he knows he is being kept alive for his usefulness. The Count needs an Englishman who can describe England, complete the purchase of a house, and then be consumed when the paperwork is done. Jonathan finds his own clothes locked away and a traveler's letters already written in a version of his handwriting, dated ahead, so that no one in England will look for him until the Count is gone. He searches for a way out. The doors to the outside are locked. The windows open onto a drop that would kill him.

He watches the Count load the boxes of earth onto a cart. Gypsies, the Szgany who serve the castle and do not meet Jonathan's eyes, haul them away. The Count is leaving for England inside a box of his native soil, one of fifty. A vampire, Jonathan pieces this together from fear and from an old book in the library, must rest in the earth of his home. Dracula is shipping his bedrooms to London.

On the last day, knowing he will be given to the three women if he stays, Jonathan climbs from a window down the castle wall. He would rather fall than remain. The journal breaks off. Between this page and the next time we see him, there is a long darkness. He will turn up in a hospital in Budapest, raving of wolves and blood, with his hair gone white in places. Mina will be sent for. He will marry her there, hastily, and put the journal in her hands, ashamed of it, unsure whether he is mad. She will seal it and not read it. Love, at that moment, looks like refusing to open a document. Later, love will look like the opposite.

# Mina and Lucy

While Jonathan is in the castle, Mina Murray is in England, and the book opens a second life.

Mina is a schoolmistress, practical, affectionate, and modern in a way the men around her underestimate. She is learning shorthand and typing so she can help Jonathan in his work. She goes to the seaside town of Whitby, on the Yorkshire coast, to stay with her friend Lucy Westenra. Lucy is beautiful, wealthy, a little dreamy, and loved by three men at once, which she reports to Mina in letters that are half guilt and half delight.

The three suitors matter, because all three will still be in the story when the charm of the letters is gone. Doctor John Seward runs a lunatic asylum in London and records his cases on phonograph cylinders. He loves Lucy and is refused. Quincey Morris, a Texan, frank and brave, loves Lucy and is refused. Arthur Holmwood, later Lord Godalming, loves Lucy and is accepted. The three men stay friends. Stoker is careful about this. The rivalry does not curdle. It becomes a fellowship, which the book will need.

Lucy begins to sleepwalk. Mina, worried, follows her one night and finds her on a churchyard seat above the harbor, a dark figure bent over her. Mina thinks it is a trick of the moonlight and a long cloak. When she reaches Lucy, her friend is alone, pale, and breathing strangely. Two small marks are on her throat. Mina blames a pin. She covers Lucy more carefully after that. She locks the window. She does the sensible things, and the sensible things are not quite enough, because she does not yet know what she is trying to keep out.

Whitby gives the novel its great arrival. A storm drives a ship into the harbor, the Demeter, out of Varna. The captain is dead, tied to the wheel, a crucifix in his hands. The log tells of a voyage in which the crew vanished one by one, of a thing in the hold, of fog and fear. A great dog, or a wolf, leaps from the wreck as it grounds and runs up the cliff and is gone. The cargo is fifty boxes of earth, consigned to a house in England. The boxes are delivered. Count Dracula has landed, and he has landed near Lucy.

Mina is called away to Budapest to marry the ruined Jonathan. She leaves Lucy with relief, thinking the sea air and Arthur's love will finish the recovery. She is wrong. The danger stays in Whitby after the friend who was watching leaves.

# Lucy's Illness

Lucy declines in a pattern no ordinary doctor can fit. She is pale. She is weak. She sleeps heavily and wakes weaker. The marks on her throat do not heal. Arthur writes to Doctor Seward in despair. Seward comes, sees that he is out of his depth, and sends for his old teacher from Amsterdam. Professor Abraham Van Helsing.

Van Helsing is the mind the book has been waiting for. He is old enough to trust observations that younger men would be embarrassed to write down, and modern enough to use transfusion, diagnosis, and a notebook. He looks at Lucy and does not say the word he is thinking. He asks for garlic. He hangs the flowers around the room and at the neck. He cannot, at first, make the English friends understand that he is not practicing quaint folklore. He is closing a door.

Lucy rallies. Then the defenses fail, sometimes because a well-meaning person removes them. Lucy's mother, frightened and unwell, takes the garlic away because the smell disturbs the patient. A wolf, escaped as the papers tell it, crashes the window of the bedroom one night. The shock kills the mother. Lucy is found in the morning between life and death, the wounds at her throat open again, the room full of the failure of every ordinary precaution.

The men give her their blood. Arthur first, then Seward, then Van Helsing, then Quincey. Stoker lingers on the transfusions because they are an intimacy and a sacrifice, and because they do not work. Blood given in love is not the same weapon as blood taken by the thing that is emptying her. Lucy has a last lucid interval. She asks Van Helsing to kiss her, and something in the ask is wrong, sweet in a way that is not hers. He draws back. He knows the change has begun before the death is official.

Lucy dies. Arthur is shattered. The mother is already gone. The funeral is decent, English, and useless. Van Helsing, to Seward's confusion, places a golden crucifix in Lucy's mouth and garlic in the coffin, and then, when he is briefly away, those protections are removed by people who think they are tidying a beloved body. He returns, sees the lapse, and his face is the face of a man watching a lock being opened on purpose.

Children start to come home late on the heath, with wounds at the throat, telling of a beautiful lady they call the Bloofer Lady. Seward wants this to be a separate sadness. Van Helsing takes him to the churchyard at night.

# The Bloofer Lady

What they find is Lucy, and it is not Lucy.

She moves through the graves with a grace she never had in life, a child held against her, her mouth red, her sweetness turned into an invitation. When she sees Arthur among the men, she calls to him in a voice that uses love as bait. She asks him to come to her. Arthur steps forward, because grief is a kind of obedience, and Van Helsing stops him with a crucifix. The figure recoils. The child is taken from her. She slips back into the tomb as if the dark were a door she owned.

Van Helsing spends the daylight hours making four men believe one intolerable fact. Arthur, Seward, Quincey, and he himself must open the coffin. Lucy's body will look uncorrupted, too beautiful, a week and more after death. The next night they must free her. The method is the old one, and Van Helsing does not soften it. A stake through the heart, the head removed, garlic in the mouth. He asks Arthur, who loved her most, to be the one who strikes, because love should be the hand that gives her rest.

They do it. The scene is dreadful, and Stoker writes it as a rescue, not as a desecration. The thing that was wearing Lucy's body snarls, the white teeth and the blood. Arthur drives the stake. The screaming thing passes. What remains in the coffin is Lucy as they knew her, calm at last, the mockery gone from the face. They finish the rite. They walk out of the churchyard as men who have crossed a line they cannot explain to the police, the church, or the newspapers. They also walk out as men who trust Van Helsing completely. The professor has been right too many times to be argued with.

He tells them the harder truth. Lucy was not the source. She was a victim who became a weapon. The source is the buyer of the fifty boxes, the passenger in the hold of the Demeter, the nobleman who has taken a house at Carfax, next door to Seward's asylum. Count Dracula is in London, and London is a table set for him. Millions of people. Narrow streets. A city that does not believe in him, which is his best camouflage.

# The Papers Come Together

Mina and Jonathan return to England as a married couple, Jonathan still frail, Mina still protecting him from his own journal. In Exeter, and then in the circle of Lucy's friends, the separate documents begin to meet.

Mina learns that Lucy is dead. The manner of the death is kept from her at first. She is not a person who can be protected by ignorance for long. She meets Van Helsing. He has heard enough about Jonathan's illness, the white hair, the raving about a castle, to suspect that the young solicitor has already been inside the enemy's house. He asks Mina for the journal. She gives it to him. He reads it and weeps, not only from horror but from gratitude, because a careful woman's record has handed him the enemy's methods, his route, and his weakness.

Mina does the thing she was training for in the first chapters, and it turns out to be the most important work in the novel. She types the diaries. She copies the phonograph cylinders into text. She puts letters, clippings, and ship's logs into order. She makes one story out of five frightened witnesses. Van Helsing, who has underestimated her in the way good men of his time underestimate a capable woman, sees what she has done and treats her as a partner. For a while the group is complete. Jonathan, Mina, Arthur, Quincey, Seward, Van Helsing. They know the Count's London addresses. They know he must have his boxes of earth. They plan to find the boxes and make them useless to him, sterilizing the soil with the sacred host so that a vampire cannot rest there.

There is a witness they still treat as a patient rather than as a document. In Seward's asylum, next door to Carfax, a man named Renfield has been eating flies, then spiders, then birds, begging for a kitten, always seeking a life he can consume in order to prolong his own. He is classified as a zoophagous madman, a life-eater. His spells of agitation match, if anyone had been looking, the movements of the new neighbor. Renfield knows when the Count is near. He lets the Count in one night, because he has been promised a greater life, and then, in a flash of sanity and pity, he tries to resist. He fails. He is found dying, his back broken, his plea to see Mina dismissed until it is too late. His last clear words are a warning about her. The men, busy with heroism, arrive after the warning has already come true.

# Mina's Scar

The attack on Mina happens in the room where Jonathan lies in a drugged sleep, and it is the scene the rest of the hunt answers to.

The men burst in. Dracula is there. He has opened a vein in his own chest and forced Mina to drink. Her mouth is stained. Her face, when the Count is driven back by their crucifixes and their fury, is a face of humiliation as much as pain. Dracula cannot be killed in that room, not by them, not that night. He leaves as a presence and a threat. He tells Mina, in so many words, that she is his now, that the men will come to hate the touch of her, that she is flesh of his flesh. He vanishes.

Mina asks Van Helsing to touch her forehead with a piece of the sacred wafer, the host he carries as both priestly object and weapon. He does. It burns her. A scar remains, white and terrible, as if the holiness itself has refused her. She does not scream only from the pain. She screams because she has been marked, in front of her husband, as belonging partly to the thing they are hunting. From this night she carries a link she did not choose. At dawn and at sunset, if Van Helsing hypnotizes her, she can sense the Count. She can say whether he is on a ship, whether she hears water, whether she hears wolves. The link is a violation. It is also the compass that will save them.

Jonathan's love does not flinch. Stoker gives him that grace. He does not draw back from the scar. The fear that she will become what Lucy became sits in the room with all of them, unspoken and constant. Mina, clear-eyed, makes them promise that if the change takes her fully, they will give her the same mercy they gave Lucy, and they will not let her become a weapon against them. She asks for the stake if the time comes. It is the bravest request in the book, and Jonathan can barely hear it.

They go to work on the boxes. Carfax. A house in Piccadilly. Other lairs the Count has prepared so that no single raid can trap him. They break into the chapels of earth. They seal what they find. Dracula, discovering the net, moves faster than they hoped. He has money, he has the allegiance of people who will transport a coffin and ask no questions, and he has centuries of practice at leaving a country just as it learns his name. Most of the boxes are ruined. He escapes with one. He means to return to Transylvania, to the castle, where his power is oldest and where the three women wait.

Mina urges the men to think as he thinks. He is old. He is proud. He will not scatter into random hiding when he can retreat along a line he understands, a ship, a river, a road of loyal servants, home. Her compass-sense confirms it. The hunt leaves London.

# The Race

They divide, because the Count has more than one possible route and because Mina's trances give them bearings rather than a map.

Arthur and Quincey take one path. Jonathan and Seward take another. Van Helsing, who will not leave Mina unguarded and will not leave the castle standing behind them, takes Mina toward the Borgo Pass itself. The Count is on a river route toward Galatz and then into the mountains, traveling by day as cargo, a box among boxes, defended when he must rest by the Szgany who have served his house for generations. The men ride, bribe, threaten, and follow. Winter is coming down on the Carpathians. The days are short. A vampire on the road is deadly in the hours of dark and helpless in the box while the sun is up. Everything depends on reaching that box before a sunset.

Van Helsing and Mina draw near the castle. The three women come to them in the night, beautiful and horrible, calling to Mina as a sister. Mina's scar burns. She does not go to them, though the pull is real. By day Van Helsing enters the ruined chapel and does the work he came to do. He finds the tombs of the three women. He gives them the same ending Lucy was given, one by one, though his hands shake and the beauty of them makes the hammer hesitate. He also finds, and destroys, the Count's older resting places in the castle, so that even a victory on the road cannot be undone by a return to a ready grave. The castle ceases to be a home.

On the road below, the other four men catch the convoy at the edge of evening. The Szgany are armed. The sun is almost down. A box of earth is in a wagon, and the light is failing, and if the sun sets before the box is opened the thing inside will rise with all its power in its own mountains. They do not have a clean battle. They have minutes.

Jonathan and Quincey reach the wagon. Quincey is shot as he fights through, a wound in his side that he ignores because the sky is the clock that matters. They pry the lid. Dracula lies there, younger, gorged on the blood of the journey, his eyes opening as the last red light sits on the snow. Jonathan shears through the throat. Quincey drives his knife into the heart. It is not the stake-and-prayer of Lucy's tomb. It is a killing done at the last possible breath of day, with the body trying to become a man again under their hands.

The Count's face changes. The hate and the long age pass out of it. For a moment, before the body crumbles, there is a look of peace, the look of a man released from a prison he had made into a kingdom. Dust. The threat that began with a courteous host at a castle door ends as dust on the snow.

Quincey Morris settles back into Arthur's arms. He is dying, and he has time to see Mina's forehead. The scar is gone. The wafer's mark, which burned her while Dracula lived, has faded the moment he ended. Quincey says that it was worth this, to die knowing she is free. He dies there, a Texan on a Transylvanian road, and the book does not forget him.

# Seven Years Later

The novel closes with a short note, years on.

Jonathan and Mina have a son. His birthday is the day Quincey died, and his name is Quincey. The men who survived, Arthur, Seward, Van Helsing, come to the boy as godfathers of a kind, a ring of uncles bound by a story they do not tell at dinner. Mina writes that the boy's bundle of papers, the typed record she made, is the true monument. There is hardly a personal letter among the later years. There is happiness, ordinary and earned. Van Helsing, holding the child, says that he will learn one day how brave his mother is. The papers can prove it.

Jonathan adds a last line. They have been back to Transylvania. The castle is still a ruin on the rock. He looks at Mina in the sunlight, and at their son, and he can hardly believe that the things in the papers happened. The proof is not a body. The body is dust. The proof is that they are alive, and that the boy carries the name of the man who bought that life with his own.

# What the Story Leaves Behind

Dracula is a pursuit story, and it is a story about belief arriving too late.

Jonathan explains away the mirror, the wolves, the locked doors, because a modern profession has no folder for them. Mina's typing, which looks like clerical work, is the weapon that finally organizes the truth. Lucy dies because love without knowledge keeps removing the garlic. Renfield dies because a patient is not allowed to be a witness until the last hour. Mina survives because the group, at last, treats a woman's mind as part of the war, and because Quincey and Jonathan reach a box while the sun is still a fact.

The Count himself is not a random predator. He is a mind from an old world, buying houses, studying timetables, moving his earth ahead of him like a general moving supply. His mistake is the mistake of a tyrant. He thinks people are blood, property, and puppets. He does not understand a friendship that survives a refusal, a marriage that survives a scar, or a record kept carefully enough to be believed. The dust on the snow is what that misunderstanding comes to.

This is the end of the story of Dracula.
